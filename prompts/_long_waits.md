LONG WAITS (#1042, #1066) — never wait inside your session; the engine waits for you. Your prompt cache lives 5 minutes: a foreground `sleep` loop that wakes after 9-10 minutes rewrites your whole context as a cache write (150K-300K tokens, about $1 per wake on Sonnet, against about $0.08 for a cache read), and that pattern was 72% of three days' spend. A job that may run longer than about 4 minutes (a suite, a gate, a bench batch, a Windows-host run, a build): start it with `harness-wait run --deadline-s <most seconds it may take> --log <logfile> -- <command…>`, then END YOUR TURN at once: one short reply naming what you wait for (no promise, no more tool calls). `harness-wait run` starts the command detached, keeps its output in the log and its exit code in `<logfile>.rc`, and writes your wait spec; the engine checks it for you at no model cost and resumes this session ONCE, when the job exits or the deadline passes, with `wait done: <condition>, rc=<n>, log tail: …`. Judge the result from that message and read the log for more; never loop on it. Other conditions: `harness-wait set --deadline-s <s> --pid <pid>` (a process exits), `--path <file>` (a file appears), `--regex <re> --log <file>` (a line appears in a log from now on). Not allowed, whatever the Bash timeout: `for i in $(seq 1 58); do …; sleep 10; done`, `timeout 585 bash -c "until …"`, `end=$((SECONDS+570)); while …`, or a Bash `run_in_background` you then keep polling. A wait on another lane (a lock queue, a merge, a PR) is not a job you can run: park the lane where a park exists (a lock queue, #1034) instead of polling. A usage limit needs nothing from you: the engine parks the lane and resumes it once the limit has cleared. Polling you cannot avoid: one short check per wake, each wake under 270 seconds so the cache stays warm. Never a blocking loop.
